Overnight Developments

bear Amazon becomes the third hyperscaler in a row where the market punishes AI capex despite accelerating demand

Amazon reported Q3 2026 net sales of $201.9B (+12% YoY), operating income of $28.1B (13.9% margin, Amazon's highest ever), and net income of $20.7B ($1.89 diluted EPS). AWS was the headline: net sales of $46.0B, +39.3% YoY — the fastest AWS growth rate in nearly five years — at a $184B annualized run-rate, with AWS operating income of $17.8B. CEO Andy Jassy attributed the acceleration "primarily" to AI investment and said AWS expects revenue growth rates exceeding 40% in both 2027 and 2028.

The capex and cash-flow picture is the part the market reacted to. Q3 cash capex was $75.9B (SEC exhibit 99.1); trailing-twelve-month free cash flow swung to -$42.4B, versus +$14.8B in the prior-year period — the second consecutive quarter of negative FCF. 2026 cash capex guidance was held at "approximately $220 billion," and CFO Brian Olsavsky told the call Amazon expects to spend "meaningfully more" in 2027, with "a clear line of sight to attractive financial returns" but no specific number given. AMZN fell 7.20% premarket.

This is now the third hyperscaler in four trading days (after Alphabet and Microsoft on 10/28) where accelerating demand metrics were overshadowed by capex/cash-flow optics. Separately, Intel — not a hyperscaler, but one node down the stack — beat Q3 2026 non-GAAP EPS ($0.52 vs. $0.40 consensus) and posted its strongest data-center revenue growth in 15 years, but fell ~21% after-hours on a soft Q4 revenue guide ($15.0-16.0B) and investor concern about 2027 capital intensity. Four companies, four different businesses, one common thread: the market is now pricing heavy AI infrastructure spending as a risk rather than a reward, even where the underlying demand signal (AWS +39%, Azure +48%, Google Cloud +103%, Intel's best data-center growth in 15 years) keeps improving. This is a market-sentiment/financing-timeline shift, not evidence of a demand pullback — no hyperscaler cut capex this cycle (P037 context, held 92%) — but the eroding willingness of equity markets to fund AI capex without near-term FCF proof is itself a data point for the financing-fragility axis, distinct from and arguably more concrete than this cycle's financing-fragility chatter in private/neocloud credit markets.

Yesterday's report characterized Alphabet's -11.51% premarket move as "almost entirely" a non-operational SpaceX mark-to-market loss. Two corrections, sourced directly from Alphabet's SEC 8-K (Exhibit 99.1, Q3 2026): first, the stock's actual regular-session close on 10/29 was -12.6% to $283.82, wiping out roughly $500B in market capitalization — the worst single trading day in Alphabet's history (since its 2004 IPO), and the second-largest one-day market-cap loss in U.S. history after NVIDIA's -$589B drop in January 2025. Second, and more analytically important: Alphabet's Q3 2026 free cash flow was -$9.065B (capex $51.355B against operating cash flow of $42.29B) — a real, cash-basis negative figure, not an artifact of the SpaceX unrealized loss (FCF is unaffected by mark-to-market equity accounting). watch Financing-fragility axis continues: NVIDIA weighs backing Nscale's Anthropic-linked debt; Microsoft taps the bond market

The Information (10/29, via Reuters) reported NVIDIA is in talks to provide billions of dollars in credit guarantees to help Nscale fund construction of a new 460MW phase at its Monarch, West Virginia campus — doubling the capacity Anthropic has committed to under its existing six-year, ~$45B deal with Nscale (announced August; the site runs on NVIDIA's upcoming Vera Rubin chips, due late 2027). Terms aren't final. This extends the vendor-financing pattern already logged with Broadcom's $63B Anthropic chip-financing guarantee (10/27) — a second chipmaker now underwriting debt for a customer's cloud-provider counterparty, concentrating balance-sheet risk upstream at the silicon suppliers themselves.

Separately, Microsoft priced $25B in senior unsecured bonds on 10/29 — its first U.S. dollar bond sale since 2017 — drawing $115B in orders (4.6x oversubscribed) despite an AAA rating that didn't require it to tap debt markets. Proceeds are earmarked for general corporate purposes including capex. Read together with Alphabet's and Amazon's existing bond issuance this year, the pattern is that large-cap, investment-grade hyperscaler debt access remains wide open and oversubscribed even as credit for sub-investment-grade neoclouds has reportedly tightened (Musk's "guppy-level death" comments and Nebius's CEO on frozen asset-backed markets, both 10/26). That bifurcation — AAA hyperscaler debt still cheap and abundant, neocloud debt tightening — is worth tracking as a leading indicator of where financing stress would first become binding if it spreads.

The Wall Street Journal and Bloomberg (10/29-30) reported that a Megaspeed executive, Alice "Le" Huang, developed a close relationship with Jensen Huang and is suspected by U.S. intelligence of being a Chinese asset; officials reportedly briefed Trump on the matter and he did not act.
